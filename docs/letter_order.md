I had an idea, what if we don't focus on letter frequency alone, but also look at what would unlock the most words. For english it would look something like this, but not sure if it is easy to translate to other languages algorithmically without research per language.  Could also be a community effort?

Here is a rough english variant:

Stage 1 — Home row anchors (F, J, A, S, D, K, L)
Why?
establishes tactile landmarks
builds the “keyboard map”
allows early words like: as, sad, dad, lad, all
Already meaningful output on day 1.

Stage 2 — Add E, T, O, I (top-frequency vowels + consonant)
Now the child can type:

to, it, at, sat, let, lot, late, date, tail, sail
and dozens of CVC words (cat, sat, set, sit, lot, lit)

This is where the “music” starts.

Stage 3 — Add N, R, S, H (high-frequency consonants)
This unlocks:

ran, has, his, this, that, then, her, son, hot, not
early sentence building:
“this is a cat”
“he ran to it”

Now typing becomes language.

Stage 4 — Add L, D, C, U (common mid-frequency letters)
This unlocks:

could, call, cold, cut, cup, luck, duck, doll

early storytelling:
“the cat ran up”
“dad had a cup”

Stage 5 — Add punctuation + space + backspace
Now the child can write:

simple stories
messages
jokes
commands
their own name
names of family members

This is where motivation skyrockets.

Stage 6 — Add M, W, F, G, Y (expands expressive power)
Unlocks:

my, why, way, may, fog, frog, wag, yum, fun, mug

more complex sentences
early creative writing

Stage 7 — Add B, V, K, X, J, Q, Z (low-frequency letters)
These are introduced last because:

they are rare
they require more awkward reaches
they don’t unlock many early words
they can frustrate beginners
